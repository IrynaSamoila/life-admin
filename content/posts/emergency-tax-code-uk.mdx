Starting a new job is exciting until your first payslip arrives.

You expected one amount.

Instead, much more tax has been deducted than you expected, or your tax code looks completely different from the one you had before.

Your first thought is often that payroll has made a mistake.

Sometimes they have.

But much more often, nothing has actually gone wrong.

Your employer has simply not received enough information from HM Revenue & Customs (HMRC) yet, so they have had to use a temporary tax code.

This is commonly called an **emergency tax code**.

The good news is that, in many cases, it fixes itself once HMRC updates your records.

Understanding what is happening can save you a lot of unnecessary stress.

If this is your first job in the UK, it may also help to read
[Your First Adult Documents Pack](/blog/first-adult-documents-pack-uk), which explains the main documents and numbers you'll come across when you start working.

## What is an emergency tax code?

An emergency tax code is a **temporary tax code** used when HMRC or your employer does not yet have enough information to calculate your tax in the normal way.

It does **not** automatically mean something has gone wrong.

It also does **not** necessarily mean you will pay too much tax.

It simply means PAYE (Pay As You Earn) has to calculate your tax using limited information until your correct details become available.

This commonly happens when:

- you start your first UK job
- you change employers
- you return to work after a long break
- you have more than one job
- your employer has not received your previous pay information
- you did not provide a P45 and instead completed a Starter Checklist

Once HMRC receives enough information, they usually issue a new tax code to your employer.

Future payslips are then calculated using that updated code.

---

## Why does this happen?

PAYE works by collecting tax throughout the tax year rather than all at once.

To do that accurately, HMRC needs to know things like:

- how much you've already earned this tax year
- how much tax you've already paid
- whether you have another job
- whether you're receiving taxable benefits or a pension
- which employer should receive your Personal Allowance

If some of that information is missing, payroll cannot simply guess.

Instead, they must use the tax code HMRC allows for that situation.

In other words, an emergency tax code is often a **placeholder** while HMRC updates your record.

## Common situations where emergency tax codes appear

### You're starting your first job

This is one of the most common situations.

Since you have no previous employer during the current tax year, payroll may temporarily use information from your Starter Checklist until HMRC confirms your tax code.

---

### You've changed jobs

Normally your previous employer gives you a **P45** when you leave.

The P45 tells your new employer:

- how much you've earned so far this tax year
- how much tax you've already paid
- your previous tax code

If your new employer does not receive this information before running payroll, they may need to use a temporary code.

(We'll cover P45s in more detail in a future guide about **P45 vs P60 vs P11D**.)

---

### You have two jobs

Most people only receive one full Personal Allowance.

HMRC therefore has to decide which employer should apply it.

Until that happens, one employer may use a temporary code.

This can make your payslip look surprising, even though the system is working as intended.

---

### You're returning to work

If you've had a long career break, lived abroad, or stopped working for a period, HMRC may need time to rebuild your PAYE record before your permanent tax code is applied.

---

## Emergency tax code does **not** always mean you've paid too much tax

This is one of the biggest misconceptions online.

Many articles say that an emergency tax code means you've definitely overpaid tax.

That isn't always true.

An emergency tax code simply changes **how PAYE calculates tax** until your correct information is available.

Depending on your circumstances, you could temporarily:

- pay too much tax
- pay too little tax
- pay roughly the correct amount

However, overpayments are common when someone starts a new job after already working earlier in the same tax year, because payroll may not yet know how much tax has already been deducted.

If too much tax has been collected, HMRC normally corrects this once your records have been updated.

## Your NI number and your tax code are different things

People often mix these up because both appear when you start a job.

They do completely different jobs.

| Item | What it identifies |
|------|--------------------|
| National Insurance number | Your National Insurance contribution record and identity within the NI system |
| Tax code | How PAYE should calculate Income Tax from your pay |
| Payroll number | Your employer's internal payroll reference for you |
| UTR (Unique Taxpayer Reference) | Your record for Self Assessment, if you need one |

Your employer normally needs your National Insurance number.

HMRC uses your tax code to tell payroll how Income Tax should be calculated.

The two are connected to your employment record, but they are **not interchangeable**.

If you're unsure where to find your National Insurance number or why you have one, see our guide to **National Insurance Numbers in the UK** (coming soon).

## How can you tell if you're on an emergency tax code?

The easiest place to look is your payslip.

Your tax code may contain letters such as:

- **W1**
- **M1**
- **X**

These indicate that PAYE is calculating tax on a **non-cumulative basis**, meaning it looks only at the current pay period instead of considering your pay and tax across the tax year so far.

You may also see the word **NONCUM** on some payroll systems instead.

Different payroll software displays this in different ways, but they all mean broadly the same thing.

The exact tax code itself can vary from year to year.

The important part is usually the **W1**, **M1**, **X**, or **NONCUM**, rather than the numbers before them.

For example, you might see codes such as:

- 1257L W1
- 1257L M1
- 1257L X

These examples are common, but the numeric part of your tax code can change if tax allowances change in future tax years.

## Does this mean payroll made a mistake?

Usually, no.

Employers must follow the tax code they receive from HMRC or the rules that apply when they do not yet have enough information.

Payroll staff generally cannot decide to "give you a better tax code" because you ask them to.

If they knowingly use the wrong tax code, they risk calculating PAYE incorrectly.

That's why the solution is normally to make sure HMRC has the correct information, rather than asking payroll to override it.

## What should you do if your tax code looks wrong?

In many cases, the answer is surprisingly simple:

**Don't panic after your first payslip.**

Payroll often runs before HMRC has finished updating your employment record. It is common for your tax code to be corrected automatically over the next few weeks.

That said, it is still worth checking that nothing is missing.

### Step 1: Check whether your employer has the right information

Ask yourself:

- Did you give your employer your **P45** from your previous job?
- If you didn't have a P45, did you complete a **Starter Checklist**?
- Is your name, date of birth and National Insurance number correct in your employer's records?

Sometimes a tax code is temporary simply because payroll was missing one piece of information when they processed your first salary.

If you've recently changed jobs, it's also worth checking that your previous employer has issued your P45. Without it, your new employer has less information to work with.

---

### Step 2: Check your tax code with HMRC

If your payslip still doesn't look right after a short time, check what HMRC thinks your tax code should be.

The easiest way is through your **Personal Tax Account**, where you can see:

- your current tax code
- your employers
- your estimated income
- previous tax codes
- whether HMRC has recently updated your record

If HMRC's record is correct but your employer is still using an old code, payroll can usually apply the updated one once they receive it through their normal PAYE system.

---

### Step 3: Contact HMRC if something is genuinely wrong

Sometimes HMRC has incomplete or incorrect information.

For example:

- an old employer is still shown as your main job
- HMRC thinks you still have two jobs when you only have one
- your estimated income is significantly wrong
- your Personal Allowance has been allocated to the wrong employer

If that's the case, contact HMRC.

Once they update your record, they normally issue a revised tax code directly to your employer.

You usually do **not** need to calculate your own tax or ask payroll to choose a different code.

---

## How long does it take to fix an emergency tax code?

There isn't a single answer.

Many people receive a corrected tax code within a few weeks of starting work, especially if HMRC receives all the necessary information quickly.

Sometimes it happens after the next payroll run.

In more complicated situations—such as having multiple jobs, changing jobs several times in one tax year, or returning from overseas—it can take longer.

The important thing to remember is that a temporary tax code is exactly that: **temporary**.

---

## Will you get your money back if you paid too much tax?

Usually, yes.

If too much Income Tax has been deducted through PAYE, HMRC normally adjusts this automatically once your correct tax code is applied.

There are two common ways this happens.

### Your next payslip is higher

This is the most common outcome.

Once payroll receives your updated tax code, PAYE recalculates your tax for the year so far.

If you've overpaid, the extra tax is often refunded automatically through your salary.

Many people don't receive a separate payment—they simply notice that their next payslip shows less tax being deducted or includes a refund.

---

### HMRC issues a refund later

Sometimes the overpayment isn't corrected during the tax year.

If HMRC identifies that you've paid too much tax after the end of the tax year, they may send you a **P800 tax calculation** explaining the difference.

If you're due a refund, HMRC will explain how to claim it or pay it automatically, depending on your circumstances.

---

## What if you've paid too little tax?

This surprises some people.

An emergency tax code doesn't always mean you've overpaid.

Occasionally, it can result in **too little** tax being deducted.

If that happens, HMRC normally adjusts your tax code later so the remaining tax can be collected gradually through PAYE.

This is another reason not to ignore an unexpected tax code, even if your take-home pay looks higher than expected.

## Check your pension deductions at the same time

When people notice an unfamiliar tax code, they often focus only on the Income Tax section of their payslip.

It's worth checking the rest of the payslip too.

For example:

- pension contributions
- National Insurance contributions
- student loan deductions (if applicable)
- salary sacrifice arrangements

If you've just joined a workplace pension, seeing a pension deduction on your first payslip is often completely normal.

## Keep the paperwork

Once your tax code has been corrected, don't throw everything away.

Keep copies of:

- your first payslip
- later corrected payslips
- your P45 (if you received one)
- any tax code notices from HMRC
- any letters or messages confirming changes

Problems are rare, but if something doesn't add up later, having the documents makes it much easier to understand what happened.

If you already keep your important paperwork digitally, save these documents alongside your employment records.

## Can you avoid an emergency tax code?

Not always.

Sometimes it's simply part of the normal process of starting a new job.

However, you can reduce the chances by:

- giving your new employer your P45 as soon as possible
- completing a Starter Checklist accurately if you don't have a P45
- making sure HMRC knows about changes to your employment
- checking your Personal Tax Account if something doesn't look right
- keeping your employment documents organised

Even if you do everything correctly, there can still be a short delay while information moves between employers and HMRC.

That doesn't necessarily mean anyone has made a mistake.

## Tax codes you might see on your payslip

An emergency tax code isn't the only unfamiliar code you might come across.

Some tax codes simply tell payroll to calculate your tax in a particular way.

Here's a quick overview of some common ones.

| Tax code | What it generally means |
|----------|--------------------------|
| Standard tax code (varies by tax year) | You're receiving your Personal Allowance through this job. |
| **W1**, **M1** or **X** | Tax is being calculated on a non-cumulative basis using only the current pay period. These are commonly associated with emergency tax codes. |
| **BR** | All income from this job is taxed at the basic rate. This often applies to a second job where your Personal Allowance is used elsewhere. |
| **D0** | All income from this job is taxed at the higher rate. |
| **D1** | All income from this job is taxed at the additional rate. |
| **0T** | No Personal Allowance is applied. This can happen if HMRC doesn't have enough information or your allowance has already been used elsewhere. |
| **NT** | No Income Tax is deducted from this employment. |
| **K** codes | Adjustments mean taxable income is increased for PAYE purposes, usually because deductions exceed available allowances. |

Seeing one of these codes does **not** automatically mean something is wrong.

Your tax code depends on your own circumstances.

If you're unsure why you've been given a particular code, check your Personal Tax Account or contact HMRC before assuming payroll has made a mistake.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I work while I'm on an emergency tax code?

Yes.

An emergency tax code only affects how PAYE calculates your Income Tax.

It does **not** affect your right to work.

---

### Does an emergency tax code affect my National Insurance?

No.

Income Tax and National Insurance are calculated separately.

Your National Insurance contributions are based on NI rules, not on your PAYE tax code.

---

### Will my employer know that my tax code has changed?

Usually, yes.

When HMRC issues a new tax code, it is normally sent electronically to your employer so payroll can apply it in a future payroll run.

You don't usually need to hand over a paper document.

---

### Should I ask payroll to change my tax code?

Usually not.

Payroll must generally operate the tax code instructed by HMRC.

If the information held by HMRC is wrong, it's normally HMRC that needs to correct it.

---

### Can I have an emergency tax code if I have two jobs?

Yes.

Having more than one job is one of the situations where temporary or unexpected tax codes are more likely while HMRC works out how your Personal Allowance should be allocated.

---

### What if my tax code still hasn't changed?

If several pay periods have passed and you're confident the code is still wrong, check your Personal Tax Account.

If the information there is also incorrect, contact HMRC.

If HMRC's record is correct but your employer hasn't applied the updated code yet, ask your payroll department whether they've received the latest coding notice.

---

### Can I get an emergency tax code every time I change jobs?

Not usually.

Many people move between jobs without ever seeing one.

However, if your new employer doesn't have enough information when they process your first payroll, a temporary tax code may still be used until HMRC updates your record.

## Final note

Getting your first payslip can feel intimidating, especially if you're new to the UK or starting a new job after a break.

Seeing an unfamiliar tax code often makes people think they've done something wrong.

In reality, emergency tax codes are a normal part of the PAYE system when HMRC or your employer doesn't yet have all the information needed to calculate your tax in the usual way.

In many cases, the problem resolves itself once your records are updated.

The important thing is to check that HMRC has the correct information, keep your employment documents, and give the system a little time to catch up before assuming the worst.
